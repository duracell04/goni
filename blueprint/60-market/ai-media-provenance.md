# AI Media Provenance and Meta "AI info" Labels

Status: Specified only / roadmap (technology intelligence; not a product guarantee).

Evidence snapshot: 2026-08-01

This brief explains the public mechanisms Meta describes for identifying and
labeling AI-created or AI-edited media on Facebook, Instagram, and Threads. It
does not claim access to Meta's private detection rules, model thresholds, or
region-specific rollout state.

## Executive answer

Meta does not need to infer an "AI style" from appearance alone. Its published
approach combines provenance signals and platform context:

1. industry-standard metadata, including C2PA and IPTC signals;
2. invisible watermarks or related pixel-level markers;
3. direct knowledge that Meta's own generative tools were used;
4. disclosures supplied by the person or advertiser posting the media; and
5. classifiers intended to detect some generated media when markers are absent.

No one signal is complete. Metadata can be stripped, watermarks can be
degraded, classifiers can fail, and a valid provenance record does not prove
that the depicted event is true. An "AI info" label therefore means that Meta
received or detected evidence of AI involvement; it does not by itself mean
that the whole asset is fabricated.

## Signal stack

### 1. Signed and conventional metadata

Meta says it reads industry-shared indicators, including information expressed
through C2PA and IPTC standards. These indicators can identify the tool or
organization that signed a provenance record and can describe creation or edit
actions.

C2PA Content Credentials are cryptographically signed provenance assertions
bound to an asset. Signature validation can show that the assertions are
well-formed, associated with the asset, and have not been altered since
signing. It does **not** prove that the assertions exhaust the asset's history,
that the signer is infallible, or that the scene depicted is factually true.

Content Credentials are also not exclusive to AI. Cameras, news organizations,
and conventional editing software can use the same standard. The relevant
question is what the signed assertions say, not merely whether a C2PA manifest
exists.

### 2. Invisible watermarks

An invisible watermark encodes a machine-detectable signal in the media rather
than only in a detachable metadata block. Meta has publicly described using
invisible watermarking for images created with its own generative features and
developing detectors for such signals.

Watermarks can be more durable than file metadata across some resizing,
re-encoding, cropping, or screenshot workflows, but they are not indestructible.
Detection normally depends on a corresponding detector and an implementation
that recognizes the watermark family.

OpenAI's current public provenance approach similarly combines C2PA Content
Credentials with SynthID watermarks for images generated through ChatGPT,
Codex, and the OpenAI API. OpenAI also warns that failure to find either signal
does not prove that an image was not generated by its tools.

### 3. First-party product records

When media is created or significantly edited inside Meta's own generative
features, Meta can use the product event itself as evidence. It does not need to
rediscover that event from pixels after upload. Meta's advertising guidance,
for example, describes labels for ads created or significantly edited with its
generative creative tools.

This first-party evidence is different from a general-purpose detector. It says
that a known product operation occurred; it still does not determine whether
the resulting claim or scene is truthful.

### 4. User and advertiser disclosure

Meta provides disclosure paths for people posting AI-generated or digitally
altered content. Its public policy requires disclosure for certain organic
content containing photorealistic video or realistic-sounding audio that was
digitally created or altered. Separate disclosure rules apply to some
advertising categories and uses.

Disclosure is therefore part of the labeling pipeline, not evidence that Meta
always detected the media independently.

### 5. Classifiers

Meta has also described work on classifiers that look for generation-related
patterns when invisible markers are missing. A classifier can contribute a
probabilistic signal, but Meta explicitly acknowledges that it is not possible
to identify all AI-generated content and that markers may be removed.

It is inaccurate to present this classifier layer as a perfect visual test or
as the only mechanism behind a label.

## Generated versus AI-edited media

Early labeling created a category error: a real photograph could receive a
"Made with AI" label after a comparatively small AI-assisted edit. Industry
metadata correctly indicated that an AI-capable operation occurred, but the
label could be read as saying the whole image was synthetic.

Meta changed the wording to "AI info" and later refined placement:

- content detected as generated by an AI tool can receive a visible label;
- content detected as only modified or edited by AI tools can place the label
  information in the post's menu; and
- Meta may show whether the basis was an industry-shared signal or
  self-disclosure.

For ads, Meta's 2026 update describes "About this ad" as the three-dot-menu
destination for additional transparency, including AI information detected
from Meta or third-party generative tools. Exact presentation can vary by
content type, significance of the edit, presence of a photorealistic person,
region, and product rollout.

## What an "AI info" label does and does not establish

The label may establish that Meta detected or received a provenance signal
associated with generation or editing. Depending on the signal, it can be
closer to a signed production receipt than to an aesthetic judgment.

The label does not, by itself, establish that:

- every part of the asset was generated;
- the asset is deceptive, inaccurate, or unlawful;
- the asset has never been edited after the recorded action;
- the provenance chain is complete; or
- unlabeled media was made without AI.

Likewise, a missing label is not proof of human-only authorship. Technical
signals may be absent, stripped, unsupported, or below a detector's threshold.

## Written captions

Meta's public AI-labeling explanations discussed in this brief are centered on
images, video, and audio. They do not document a general system that reliably
labels ordinary post captions merely because the prose was drafted or polished
with a language model.

This is a statement about the scope of the published labeling documentation,
not a claim that Meta never analyzes text for other integrity, ranking,
advertising, or policy purposes.

## Implications for Goni

Goni should treat media provenance as evidence, not as a truth oracle:

- preserve C2PA/IPTC records when ingesting or exporting media;
- validate signatures and retain the signer, actions, timestamps, and trust
  decision as separate fields;
- distinguish `generated`, `edited`, `disclosed`, and `classifier_inferred`
  evidence instead of collapsing them into one boolean;
- record missing or stripped provenance as `unknown`, not `human_created`;
- show source and confidence when provenance affects a brief or action; and
- keep media truth assessment separate from production-history verification.

These are technology-intelligence implications only. Any normative Goni media
schema or API change requires a separate specification decision.
